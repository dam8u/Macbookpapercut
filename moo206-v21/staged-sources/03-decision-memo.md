> normal cost** of a year of VRS service credit and **43% of the current-law price**, so the veteran
> keeps most of the benefit and the Commonwealth is not selling service credit below the cost VRS
> itself states. On the authorised basis the pilot is **18.4 elections over four years, range 9.2 to
> 30.7** — measured on § 51.1-142.4 C as option (c) with the no-advance bar of clause (vii) in the
> unreduced subdivision A 1 cohort, reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3. **That count is an
> upper bound, not a measurement of the current text:** clause (viii) of
> "eligible member" is in the live draft and in no committed model run, and it is a further screen,
> so it can only refuse more. Each election certifies a mean **$106,253** of subsidy as
> § 51.1-142.4 D 2 certifies it — median $106,405, 90th percentile $152,558 — which is the quantity
> D 2 caps. **The trust's own net cost per election is a different and smaller quantity, $71,813 in
> present value on the authorised 12-month rung**, because D 2 certifies the reservation on the
> earliest qualifying retirement date and the retirement window does not move it. **Two of the
> bill's own limits turn members away before money does:** § 51.1-142.4 C refuses any election that
> would not advance the member's unreduced retirement eligibility date, and a **$170,000** per-member
> ceiling refuses the most expensive 4.74% of that cohort — and the bill still brackets `[$100,000]`,
> so the ceiling figure is directed but not yet in the text. On modelled demand, at the central
> estimate the $3,000,000 funds every election the bill admits, and at the top of the range the
> appropriation binds and rations. **The pilot's estimated net cost to the trust is $1.3 million in
> present value, range $0.66M to $2.2M — about $71,813 per participating member** — and with the
> one-time $375,000 implementation allowance the headline cost to the Commonwealth is **$1.7
> million**. **One caveat travels with that figure and is required:** the authorised rung prices
> every member retiring on the last permissible day, so it is the cheap end of the window and the
> exposure is one-sided dearer — **+7.5%** if retirement dates spread uniformly across the
> permissible dates and **+16.3%** if every member retires at the earliest. There is no local
> cost and no mandate on localities, and a denied member keeps every purchase right current law
> already gives them.

## 1. The objective

Reduce, as far as is defensible, what a Virginia veteran pays to add prior active-duty military
service to their VRS years of service — without creating an undue burden on the Commonwealth's
budget, and with a design that can actually pass.

Those two halves pull against each other, so every option here is scored on both: what the member
pays, and what the Commonwealth carries. A design that is generous and dies in a money committee
delivers nothing.

## 2. The constraint that drives the answer

Virginia requires the VRS Board to prepare a cost statement on any bill that changes a retirement
benefit (Va. Code § 30-19.1:7), and the Commonwealth's practice is that a benefit increase should
be funded.

**In the 2015–2026 record, no Virginia bill carrying a quantified new unfunded pension liability
passed.** Not one. The strongest possible veteran framing — HB 1867 in 2023, four free years of
military service for teachers — was tabled **8-0, unanimous and bipartisan**, in the House
Appropriations subcommittee. The framing bought nothing in the room that mattered.

::: key
The survivable score is not a small number. It is a **category**. A VRS statement reading "minimal
impact to unfunded liability" can survive committee; a statement that attaches a number to new
trust-fund liability has not. The task is to make the cost small, bounded and *certain* — not
merely to make our own estimate small.
:::

Two further scoring facts shape the design:

- **Retirement-date acceleration, not the price discount, is what costs money.** Purchased service
  counts toward eligibility, so a purchase moves a retirement date. Under the recommended bill the
  electing member retires about **57 months** earlier than VRS's own published rates expect. The
  member's payment does not reach that at all. Everything in §4 follows from this one fact.
- **Administration is a real line.** VRS prices implementation of a discrete § 51.1 change at
  **$104,100 – $374,500**. For a small pilot the committees can be asked to appropriate more to run
  the programme than it delivers. The package funds implementation explicitly and keeps the
  mechanism as simple as the policy allows.

**The basis note, and it is a real limitation of this table.** The scenario rows S1 to S9 are priced
on the published scenario basis — a **6-month** retirement deadline, both pathways where the design
has two. The **recommended row is on the authorised basis**: the 12-month retirement rung and the
cohort that includes option (c) and the no-advance bar. **So the recommended row is not strictly
comparable with the rows above it**, and the difference is not small: moving the scenario rows to the
12-month rung would cut each of their costs by 13–15% and change none of their participant counts,
which is measured. Re-cutting the whole table onto one rung is a decision about the package, not a
modelling result, and it has not been taken. **No verdict in this memo rests on a comparison across
that line.**

**The rounding rule, and it binds on this table.** Take-up scales a design's cost and its cohort
together, so it very nearly cancels in the per-participant ratio — the spread is about **±1%** — but
it moves every aggregate by a factor of about 3.3. **Two designs whose cost per participant falls
within the same $1,000 are therefore not ranked against each other on that measure.** On this table
that rule binds on **S3 ($57,123) against S6 ($56,900)** — 0.4% apart, well inside the take-up spread
on either figure — and on **S0 against S4**, both $47,000. The rule is the per-participant analogue
of the sign-stability rule already applied to aggregates.

goes into an external document and no S8 cost per participant may be published before reconciliation;
their participant counts are not dollar figures and are published. The retired figures $2,488,563 /

**Why the recommended design wins both halves of the test.** It reduces the member's price
substantially — to 43% of what the calendar now charges — on a basis an actuary can argue with on
the merits rather than dismiss as a subsidy with a new name. It fixes the defect instead of carving
an exception around it. And by confining the pilot to one pathway it reaches the smallest cohort of
any design that actually helps anyone — **18.4 members, range 9.2 to 30.7** — which is the honest
statement of both its strength and its cost: a small bill reaches few people. That costs the trust
**$1.32 million** in present value, **$71,813** per participating member; what the appropriation
covers is stated in §5 item 3 and in document 04 §7, in utilisation terms as well.

## 4. The recommendation

**Earned-service repricing at 6.00% statutory interest written as a fixed number, with a
$3.0 million aggregate subsidy cap drafted in the same clause set, confined to state agency
employees in Plan 1 and Plan 2, reaching only a member whom the purchase makes eligible to retire,
and requiring retirement on completion.** The pilot is **18.4 elections, range 9.2 to 30.7**, each
certifying a mean **$106,253** of subsidy as the cap clause certifies it, and costing the trust
**$1.32 million** in present value — **$71,813** per participating member.

Full design and guardrails: document 02 §4. Fiscal detail: document 04. Drafting: document 05.

### 4.1 What the veteran gets

Plan 1 member, age 55, 27 years of service, $91,700 salary, buying three years:

| | Member pays | × approximate normal cost | Saving against current law |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Current law (after the window) | $116,622 | 3.39× | — |
| **Under the bill, at 6.00%** | **$47,705** | **1.39×** | **$68,917** |
| The original draft (approximate normal cost) | $34,388 | 1.00× | $82,234 |

Across the unreduced subdivision A 1 cohort, reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3 the member pays **1.42× approximate normal cost** and **43% of the
current-law price**. On the 15 profiles nearest the crossover — the hardest cases — the member pays
**68%–77% of the current-law price, median 72%**, and **not one of them is worse off**.

### 4.2 Why 6.00%, and why the rate is not the cost control

Four of the five reasons for six percent are structural and none of them has moved:

1. It is **Virginia's own legal rate of interest**, § 6.2-301, with § 6.2-302 at the same figure.
2. At six percent the member pays **more than the cost VRS itself states** for a year of service
   credit — the one sentence the whole design rests on. At 4.00% they pay less than the original
   draft asked for, and that sentence is lost.
3. The veteran still gets a real discount: 43% of the current-law price on the bill's cohort.
4. It is **100 basis points clear** of the crossover at which the design would start costing
   long-service members more than current law.

The fifth reason — that it halved the cost of the original draft — **did not survive the model
repair and is withdrawn.** On the repaired basis the comparable ratio is 71%, and the repair
flattened the dial: across the whole usable range of rates the score now moves 109% → 59%, where it
used to appear to move 120% → 26%.

::: warn
**"Percentage of the original draft" is retired as a headline metric.** The original draft is an
unenacted document, not current law, and the repair moved both sides of the ratio together. The two
numbers that matter to a money committee are **the appropriation** and **the words VRS writes**.
:::

**Negotiating position.** 6.00% is the ask. 6.75% is acceptable **only** in trade for something
that helps the near-crossover member. 5.00% is the floor, and only with the cap clause reopened in
the same motion. **4.00% is not acceptable at any cap** — at 4% the member pays less than the
original draft and the Commonwealth pays more.

### 4.3 Where the cost reduction actually comes from

Since the cost is acceleration, the levers are the ones that move acceleration — and one of them is
in our own draft, set to its most expensive value.

| Retirement deadline after completing the purchase | Modelled cost to the trust, participation held constant |
|---|---:|
| Same month the credit posts | $1.57M |
| **As drafted — first of the month after the credit posts** | **$1.55M** |
| 6 months | $1.45M |
| **12 months — the authorised rung** | **$1.32M** |
| 18 months | $1.19M |
| 24 months | $1.06M |

A 24-month deadline is **32% below** the drafted setting **on this ladder**, changes the member's
price by nothing, and still requires the member to retire. **Read the 32% for exactly what it is.**
It is a modelled conditional result: the ladder prices one design at six settings of one parameter,
holding the participant count constant — and that constancy is **measured**, the count being
identical at every rung to ten decimal places. What no source produces is a participant count for a
*behavioural* response to the deadline. So it is **not** a demonstrated 32% reduction in programme
expenditure or in the appropriation, and a longer deadline may well change who elects. The authorised
12-month rung, $1.32M, is **15%** below the drafted setting on the same ladder. The two arguments against a longer deadline are
both weak: the windfall case is the *cheap* case, not the expensive one, and enforcement is already
solved in the draft, which re-values the credit at the current-law price and refunds the difference
if the member does not retire. **This is open decision 1 in §8.**

## 5. Decisions taken, and the reason for each

1. **Statutory interest rate: 6.00%, fixed, written as a number.** §4.2.
2. **Never draft the rate as "the Board's assumed rate of return."** It floats, it is 6.75% today,
   and at 7.00% the bill becomes worse than doing nothing for the members it is most meant to help.
3. **Aggregate cap $3.0M, drafted in the same clause set as the rate**, as an admission rule with a
   certified reservation — not as a reporting limit. **At the central estimate the $3,000,000 funds
   every election the bill admits, and at the top of the range the appropriation binds and rations.**
   On modelled demand the appropriation is **39% / 78% / 130%** committed at low, central and high
   take-up, with the `[20]`% contingency inside it — modelled demand against the cap, not observed
   utilisation. **Every claim that the cap "does not bind on any case we modelled", that it "covers
   the high case", or that "$3.0M holds at double take-up", is withdrawn.** A cap that rations at the
   top of the range has to be described as one.
4. **Scope: state agency employees only.** Removes an order of magnitude of exposure and the entire
   local-mandate fight. And no tier within it is costless — Plan 2 members are about 7% of the
   cohort and reach eligibility by a different rule.
5. **One pathway only.** A member already eligible for an unreduced benefit is excluded — the
   date the member must reach is the unreduced retirement eligibility date, which the bill
   defines as reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3, or, on an involuntary separation or a lawfully abolished office, through subsection A or C of § 51.1-155.1.
   Neither of those two routes carries weight in the modelled distribution. That closes the windfall channel and it is most of why the unreduced subdivision A 1 cohort, reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3
   is **18.4 rather than 79.2**.
6. **The post-retirement clawback is removed entirely**, and replaced by settlement at retirement
   with re-valuation at the current-law price if the member does not retire. Virginia has no
   clawback precedent, no collection mechanism, and a due-process problem on death, disability,
   layoff and transfer.
7. **The retirement condition stays, and is not presented as a cost control.** It is what makes
   this a workforce measure rather than a pension giveaway. Its *length* is open — see §8.
8. **No savings claim anywhere in the package.** Document 02 §6.
9. **The reduction-waiver alternative (S9) is demoted and is no longer the named fallback — on
   coverage, not on price.** The earlier ground for demoting it, that it was "21% dearer", is
   **withdrawn**: on the authorised basis S9 costs **$0.93M, 18% of repaired S1**, which is 55% below
   **S2** in aggregate and 19% below S2 per participant, against **S2's 23.3 elections**. It is
   demoted because it reaches **13.0** — 13.0 against S2's 23.3, and 13.0 against the scenario set's
   comparison basis, **S8 at 6.00%, at 62.2**, whose aggregate and cost per participant are
   `[WITHHELD — S8 publication restriction]`. It is cheap because it reaches the fewest
   people, which is the weakest possible answer to a veteran and not a saving the Commonwealth should
   want. **The fallback, if the score comes back unaffordable, is a longer retirement deadline on the
   same bill** — a parameterisation the patron can offer as an amendment without redrafting, priced
   on the ladder in §4.3, with the conditional reading that section states.
10. **The status-quo cost row is withheld**, and becomes a reconciliation question for VRS.
11. **The Hybrid exclusion is recorded as a scoring choice, not a principle.**

## 6. Implementation path

**The Joint Leadership Council route for 2027 is closed.** The JLC set its 2027 package on 10 June
2026 and ranked it on 16 September 2026; the eight published initiatives contain no VRS measure.
Two tracks therefore run in parallel.

**Track 1 — the 2027 session.** A patron's bill through a money-committee member, with individual
veterans organisations testifying in their own name. Retirement bills go straight to the money
committees: House Appropriations (Compensation and Retirement subcommittee) and Senate Finance and
Appropriations. Companion bills prefiled the same week in both chambers is the only § 51.1-142.2
pattern in the record that produced unanimous passage.

## 7. Principal risks

Full register, with both adversarial reviews and their status: **document 07**. The four that would
actually kill the bill:

**R-A. VRS scores the retirement condition as a plan-wide assumption change.** This is the largest
risk in the file. On the HB 1306 (2026) precedent, a provision conditioning a benefit on a
retirement date may be scored by changing a retirement-pattern assumption for everyone rather than
by counting the 18.4 people who participate. A cap does not limit an assumption change.
**Mitigation:** ask VRS directly on 12 November 2026 what structure avoids an assumption change,
and be prepared to lengthen or restructure the condition rather than lose the bill.

**R-B. The "it is not a subsidy" argument fails on contact with an actuary.** The reviewer's
verdict was blunt: a formula price below the cost of the benefit it creates is still below the cost
of the benefit it creates. **The package has dropped that claim.** What survives is narrower and
true: the price has a principled basis, the member pays more than VRS's own stated cost of a year
of credit, the gap is bounded by an appropriation, and the Commonwealth is asked to fund it
knowingly.

**R-C. Our estimate is ours.** No VRS quote exists for any member in this cohort, and the model
values the basic pension only — excluding the Health Insurance Credit, Group Life and survivor
loading, all of which VRS would include and all of which push the cost **up**.

**R-D. The administrative cost exceeds the benefit in the first biennium.** It does: $375,000 of
VRS implementation against zero benefit cost in FY2029 and FY2030. **Mitigation:** say it first. It
is the clearest evidence that the measure is small, and it is the kind of candour that earns a
"minimal impact" statement.
