## Fiscal

### 1. "You are selling service credit below its actuarial value. The difference is not a discount; it is an unfunded liability with a beneficiary."

**That is correct, and the bill does not deny it.** The price is below full actuarial-equivalent
cost. What the bill does is make the gap **bounded, certified and appropriated**: VRS must certify
an available reservation covering the member's full estimated incremental cost before accepting the
election, and the total of those certifications may not exceed the figure in the enactment clause.
The Commonwealth is being asked to fund an enhancement knowingly, within a number it chooses, not
to accept that the enhancement is free.

Note also what current law already is. Virginia sells the same service at approximate normal cost —
below actuarial cost — to any member who elects inside 24 months of hire. The question in front of
the General Assembly is not whether to create a subsidy. It is whether the existing subsidy should
continue to turn on the hire date.

### 2. "What is the maximum this can cost the Commonwealth?"

**$3.0 million of pension subsidy across the four-year pilot, plus up to $375,000 of one-time VRS
implementation.** That is the whole exposure, because the cap is an admission rule rather than a
reporting limit: VRS must certify and reserve the money before accepting an election, and when the
appropriation is exhausted it stops accepting them.

**We state a total cost, and you should know what moves it.** The pilot's net cost to the trust is
**$1.3 million** in present value, range **$0.66M to $2.2M**, or **$1.7 million** with the one-time
$375,000 of VRS systems work. The two aggregates that used to be withheld behind that figure were
never two estimates of one thing — they are two rungs of one ladder on a superseded cohort, and the
whole gap between them is retirement-date timing. **The caveat that does matter: the authorised rung
prices every member retiring on the last day the bill allows, so it is the cheap end.** A uniform
spread of retirement dates is **7.5% dearer** and all-at-the-earliest **16.3% dearer**, and no
published distribution of retirement dates inside a VRS window exists. Per election: each certifies a
mean **$106,253** of subsidy as the bill's cap clause certifies it, there are **18.4** of them on the
central estimate (range 9.2 to 30.7), and on modelled demand that commits **39% / 78% / 130%** of the
$3,000,000 at low, central and high take-up with the `[20]`% contingency inside it.

So: **at the central estimate the appropriation funds every election the bill admits, and at the top
of the range it binds and rations.** A cap that rations has to be described as one, and at high
take-up this one does. Earlier versions of this package said the appropriation *"is not exhausted"*
and *"covers the high case"*; both are **withdrawn**, because they were measured against cost to the
trust and subsection D 2 caps certified subsidy — a different quantity.

**And the appropriation is not the first thing that turns a member away.** The bill's own limits do
that. Subsection C refuses an election that would not advance the member's unreduced retirement
eligibility date — the date the member must reach, which the bill defines as reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3, or, on an involuntary separation or a lawfully abolished office, through subsection A or C of § 51.1-155.1.
Neither of those two routes carries weight in the modelled distribution. And the $170,000 per-member ceiling refuses the most expensive 4.74% of the unreduced subdivision A 1 cohort, reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3.
Between them they refuse about **2.2 of a 20.65** modelled population at the central
estimate — about 1.0 by the eligibility bars, about 1.2 by the ceiling — where the appropriation
refuses none.

**A refusal is not a trim, and the ceiling should not be sold as one.** When the ceiling catches an
election it does not shave the excess off the top: the member is refused and the Commonwealth
certifies nothing. On the refused profile the whole **$183,577** of subsidy goes, against **$9,147**
of excess over the ceiling — wrong by a factor of twenty. The ceiling is a bound on the
Commonwealth's exposure per election, not a saving of the amount by which an election is too
expensive.

### 3. "Earlier drafts of this analysis quoted different numbers. Which are right?"

The figures in this package are the ones on the **repaired** model, dated 11 October 2026. A repair
to the model's cohort and retirement-date machinery replaced an assumed six months of retirement
acceleration with VRS's own published retirement and termination rates, and rebuilt the cohort
monthly from membership history.

Document 04 §13 lists every superseded figure and why. The two that moved most: the claim that the
design "costs half of the original draft" is withdrawn, and the status-quo cost row is withheld
pending a baseline reconciliation with VRS. **The repair moved our estimates up, not down, and we
would rather publish that than be corrected in committee.**

### 4. "We just took tens of millions out of VRS contribution rates and spent it. Why should we re-obligate it?"

Because of the size and the shape of this ask. **For its first two fiscal years the bill costs
$375,000 of non-general fund money and nothing else** — the systems charge lands in FY2029, a year
in which no election can be made, and the first benefit cost falls in FY2031. The largest single
number in the first biennium is administration, not benefit.

This is not a contribution-rate measure. It is a small, capped, four-year appropriation with a
sunset.

### 5. "Does it pay for itself through earlier retirements?"

**No, and the package does not claim it does.** At central assumptions the workforce effect is
worth **+$46,037** per participant after recruitment and ramp-up cost — a real number — against a
**net cost to the trust of $64,678 per participant**. That figure's identity is confirmed: it is the
same quantity as the scoreboard's cost per participant for the original draft. But it is **not** the
pension cost of the purchased service — only **28%** of it is service credit, and **72%** is the
member retiring earlier — and it is the **original draft's** figure, not the recommended design's.
The recommended design costs **$71,813** per participant, $7,135 more, **so the exchange on the
design we recommend is worse than the 71% suggests.** It covers about **71%** of the original
draft's cost and no case we regard as central covers all of it. Break-even would need about 45
months of genuine acceleration at an 18% replacement differential.

The independent-audit record is consistent: Pennsylvania −$12.4M, −$198M and −$68M across three
early-retirement programmes; New York −$50M; West Virginia +$10M in annual retirement costs.

What the design does deliver is a transition that is **dated, controlled and reported** rather than
unmanaged, with an evaluation built to test the offset. If the evaluation says the offset is not
there, the sunset handles it. **The bill carries no backfill control** — subsection J was dropped on
11 October 2026, and a re-employment and contractor bar is what remains. The turnover argument rests
on behaviour, not on a provision, and we would rather say so than claim a mechanism the text does
not contain.

### 6. "Will VRS score this by changing a retirement-pattern assumption, the way it scored HB 1306 at $2.7 billion?"

**This is the single largest risk to the bill and we will not pretend otherwise.** HB 1306 (2026)
merely shortened a break-in-service period, and VRS's statement priced it by changing a plan-wide
assumption rather than by counting participants.

Four features distinguish this bill: coverage confined to state agency employees rather than the
teacher plan; eligibility confined to about nineteen members whom the purchase makes eligible to
retire; a certified per-member and aggregate admission control; and no liberalisation of
return-to-work rules. Whether those are sufficient is a question for the VRS actuary, and it should
be asked **before** the bill is drafted. That is why the 12 November 2026 Benefits and Actuarial
Committee meeting matters more than any other date on the calendar.

**And there is a lower-cost setting of the same bill available as an amendment.** Lengthening the
retirement deadline from "immediately" to 24 months is **32% below** the drafted setting on the
priced ladder in document 04 §6, without changing the member's price by a dollar and without
removing the requirement that the member retire. The patron can offer that without redrafting.
**Read the 32% precisely:** it is a modelled reduction in present-value cost to the trust with the
participant count held constant — not a demonstrated 32% cut in programme expenditure, and not a cut
in the appropriation, which is $3,000,000 either way. A longer deadline may change who elects, and
that effect is not modelled.

## Design

### 7. "Why six percent? Isn't that a number you picked?"

Six percent is Virginia's own legal rate of interest, Va. Code § 6.2-301, and the judgment rate
under § 6.2-302. It was selected, not invented, and here is the full reasoning:

| Rate | Why not |
|---|---|
| 4.00% — Pennsylvania's own statutory rate | The member would pay **less** than the original draft asked for, and the Commonwealth would pay **more**. A discount on a discount. |
| 5.00% | Workable, but it weakens the fairness case and reopens the cap clause. |
| **6.00%** | **Recommended.** The member pays 1.42× approximate normal cost — more than VRS's own stated cost of a year of service credit — and 43% of the current-law price. |
| 6.75% — today's assumed rate of return | Lower cost to the Commonwealth, but it takes the hardest-hit member from a 23% discount to about 6%. **We do not put a dollar comparison beside this**: every absolute S8 figure is withheld from external documents and its cost per participant may not be published before reconciliation, and a cheapness claim without a participant count and a cost per participant beside it is a claim about coverage dressed as a claim about price. |
| "the Board's assumed rate of return" | **Never.** It floats. At 7.00% the bill would exceed current law for its longest-serving beneficiaries — 25 basis points above today's figure. The draft answers this directly by charging the **lesser** of the new price and the current-law price, so no member can ever be worse off. |

**And a caution against over-claiming for the rate.** It is not the Commonwealth's main cost
control. The cost of this design is dominated by members retiring earlier than VRS expects, which
the member's payment does not reach. The levers that do reach it are the retirement deadline, the
completion requirement and the cohort.

### 8. "You say the bill takes the calendar out of the price. Does it?"

**Not entirely, and we would rather say so than be caught.** Because 6% interest exceeds the 3.50%
salary growth VRS assumes for long-service members, the price still rises about **2.4% for each
year since hire**. A 28-year veteran pays about **1.55×** what an otherwise identical 3-year veteran
pays for the same year of service — and the draft caps that at the current-law price, so it can
never exceed what the member faces today.

The accurate claim is that the bill **replaces a cliff with a ramp**. Today the price jumps 3.2× on
a single day with no gradation at all. Under the bill it rises gradually, from a base roughly half
of today's, and the gradient has a reason behind it: longer deferral, more interest.

### 9. "Why only veterans? A teacher who transferred from Maryland faces the same 24-month cliff."

**There is no clean equity answer, and we will not invent one.** The cliff is identical for all
eight non-military purchase categories, and a member who wants it fixed for everyone is making a
coherent argument.

What we have instead are two specific reasons. The first is a **workforce** reason: military
service produces a documented late-entry pattern into state employment, so the 24-month window
systematically misses veterans in a way it does not miss other groups. The second is a **fiscal**
reason: veterans are about 6% of the classified workforce, which makes a capped pilot scoreable;
fixing the cliff for all eight categories is not a capped pilot and could not be scored as one.

If the General Assembly prefers to fix the cliff for everyone, that is a larger and more expensive
bill, and this analysis does not price it.

### 10. "Why not include Hybrid plan members? They are 54% of the system."

**The exclusion is a scoring choice, not a principle, and the package says so in writing.** Plan 1
and Plan 2 are closed populations. That is precisely what makes a four-year capped pilot possible
to score. Extending the measure to an open population makes the eligible group open-ended and
weakens the argument that the cap is meaningful.

The Hybrid variant has been priced so that the cost of adding it by amendment is known in advance,
rather than discovered after the amendment is adopted.

### 11. "Why only members whom the purchase makes eligible to retire? The veteran hired at 34 is 38 now and gets nothing for twenty years."

**This is the sharpest question in the file and it comes from our own side.** The bill reaches only
a member whom the purchase makes eligible to retire now, so the mid-career veteran who was priced
out by the cliff gets no relief until they are much older.

The honest answer has two parts. First, the retirement condition is what makes this a workforce
measure rather than a general benefit increase, and the retirement condition necessarily restricts
the measure to members near retirement. Second, a pilot confined to a near-retirement cohort is
scoreable; one open to every active member is not — we priced that variant and it roughly doubles
the eligible pool.

If the General Assembly wants to reach the mid-career veteran, the mechanism is to **drop the
retirement condition and extend the price to all active Plan 1 and Plan 2 members**. We have priced
that too: it reaches about four times as many members and costs about three and a half times as
much. It is a bigger bill and it loses the workforce rationale — but it is a coherent bill and we
would not argue against anyone who wanted it.

### 12. "Won't people game it — arrange their affairs to capture the price and then not retire, or come back as a contractor?"

The first bill is designed around exactly those two channels.

- **The windfall channel is closed by the eligibility definition.** A member already eligible for
  an unreduced benefit — who could have satisfied every condition while retiring on a date they had
  already chosen — is excluded from the bill entirely. The date the member must reach is the
  unreduced retirement eligibility date, which the bill defines as reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3, or, on an involuntary separation or a lawfully abolished office, through subsection A or C of § 51.1-155.1.
- **The member must retire** on completion of the purchase, and credit is settled at retirement
  rather than created first and collected on later.
- **A re-employment and contractor bar** reaches indirect return through a staffing vendor, which
  is the hole in most states' versions.
- **One election per member**, and the purchase is limited to the months necessary to reach
  unreduced eligibility.

### 13. "Your cap creates a queue. Is a first-come, first-served subsidy fair, and is it legal?"

**The queue binds at the top of the modelled range, so treat it as live rather than theoretical.** On
modelled demand the appropriation is 78% committed at the central estimate and **130% at high
take-up** — at which point it rations and the queue does the rationing. It is also not the first
thing that refuses a member: the bill's eligibility bars and the $170,000 ceiling refuse about 2.2 of
a 20.65 population before the money refuses anyone. But the queue is drafted, it does bind in the high case,
and it has to be defensible there.

A queue is a real equity problem: an applicant whose agency produces records in a day beats one
whose agency takes six weeks, and the difference has nothing to do with merit. The bill should
therefore publish notice, accept applications through accessible channels, define a complete
application, set an objective tie-breaker, allow a cure period, give written decisions, and provide
review. An initial application batch resolved together — rather than minute-by-minute arrival
order — is worth considering.

The draft does all of that, and it adds the sentence that matters most: **a denied or wait-listed
applicant keeps every right they have today to purchase the service at the current-law price, and
the Board must say so in the notice.** That is what makes rationing defensible.

On legality: rationing a limited appropriation has a substantial rational-basis defence, and
first-come access is not facially impermissible. We have not found a controlling decision on a
pension-purchase queue, and the package does not claim one.

## Process and precedent

### 14. "Why should we believe this will not be extended forever, like every other pilot?"

You should not take the sunset alone on trust — Pennsylvania extended one of its early-retirement
programmes five times in twelve years. That is why the bill carries **three separate devices**: a
four-year sunset, a reenactment clause requiring the General Assembly to vote again affirmatively,
and an **actuarial kill switch** that suspends new admissions automatically if the actuary
certifies a need for contributions the General Assembly declines to appropriate.

All three are proven on enacted Virginia acts or on enacted acts elsewhere. The kill switch is the
one that does not depend on legislative attention.

### 15. "Four VRS benefit bills were continued last session. Why is this one different?"

It is different in four respects, and any one of them failing is a reason to continue this bill too:

1. **It is capped at a dollar figure**, not at a participant count or a class definition.
2. **It touches no locality and no teacher**, so there is no unfunded mandate and nothing for local
   government to score.
3. **It does not grant service credit.** The member pays, and pays more than VRS's own stated cost
   of a year of service credit — 1.42× the approximate normal cost rate — and never more than the
   price current law would charge them.
4. **It repairs a defect rather than adding an occupation.** The continued bills expanded benefits
   occupation by occupation. This one changes a price basis that the Commonwealth itself chose and
   that is, on the comparative evidence, an outlier.

That is the case. It is not a guarantee, and the committee's instinct to batch-dispose of VRS
benefit bills is a real risk to it.

## Three things never to say about this bill

::: warn
1. **"It is not a subsidy."** It is a price below actuarial cost. Saying otherwise invites an
   actuary to say the opposite, in writing, in the fiscal impact statement.
2. **"It pays for itself."** The audit record on state early-retirement incentives will be read
   back to you, and it is unanimous.
3. **"Virginia is the stingiest state."** Seven of the twenty states we verified charge full
   actuarial cost. The defensible claim is narrower: Virginia is the only verified state that
   withdraws a preferential price on a calendar measured from hire.
:::
