> **Reprice military-service purchase off the service instead of the calendar — total normal cost on the member's first 36 months of state pay plus 6.00% statutory interest, no election window, state agency employees only — and appropriate $3.0 million as a hard cap on the Commonwealth's share for a four-year pilot beginning 1 July 2029.** At 6.00% the member pays **1.42× the approximate
> normal cost** of a year of VRS service credit and **43% of the current-law price**, so the veteran
> keeps most of the benefit and the Commonwealth is not selling service credit below the cost VRS
> itself states. On the authorised basis the pilot is **18.4 elections over four years, range 9.2 to
> 30.7** — measured on § 51.1-142.4 C as option (c) with the no-advance bar of clause (vii) in the
> unreduced subdivision A 1 cohort, reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3. **That count is an
> upper bound, not a measurement of the current text:** clause (viii) of
> "eligible member" is in the live draft and in no committed model run, and it is a further screen,
> so it can only refuse more. Each election certifies a mean **$106,253** of subsidy as
> § 51.1-142.4 D 2 certifies it — median $106,405, 90th percentile $152,558 — which is the quantity
> D 2 caps. **The trust's own net cost per election is a different and smaller quantity, $71,813 in
> present value on the authorised 12-month rung**, because D 2 certifies the reservation on the
> earliest qualifying retirement date and the retirement window does not move it. **Two of the
> bill's own limits turn members away before money does:** § 51.1-142.4 C refuses any election that
> would not advance the member's unreduced retirement eligibility date, and a **$170,000** per-member
> ceiling refuses the most expensive 4.74% of that cohort — and the bill still brackets `[$100,000]`,
> so the ceiling figure is directed but not yet in the text. On modelled demand, at the central
> estimate the $3,000,000 funds every election the bill admits, and at the top of the range the
> appropriation binds and rations. **The pilot's estimated net cost to the trust is $1.3 million in
> present value, range $0.66M to $2.2M — about $71,813 per participating member** — and with the
> one-time $375,000 implementation allowance the headline cost to the Commonwealth is **$1.7
> million**. **One caveat travels with that figure and is required:** the authorised rung prices
> every member retiring on the last permissible day, so it is the cheap end of the window and the
> exposure is one-sided dearer — **+7.5%** if retirement dates spread uniformly across the

> cost and no mandate on localities, and a denied member keeps every purchase right current law

## 1. Current law, stated precisely

Va. Code § 51.1-142.2(B)(2) allows any member in service to purchase **up to four years** of credit
for prior active-duty military service, provided the discharge was not dishonorable. Subsection
(B)(3) sets the price in a single sentence that governs the whole of subsection B:

> For each year or portion thereof to be credited at the time of purchase under this subsection, the
> member shall pay the approximate normal cost of the retirement plan under which the member is
> covered at the time of such purchase, as determined by the Board in its sole discretion. **If the
> member does not purchase, or enter into a purchase of service credit contract for, the service
> made available in this subsection within the first 24 months of the member's active service
> following his first date of hire** … **then** … **the member shall pay the actuarial equivalent
> cost.**

Three facts follow, and all three matter.

**The price turns on the hire date alone.** Nothing else in the sentence moves it — not the length
of the service, not the member's age, not the rank or the branch, not whether the member knew the
option existed.

**The purchased service counts toward retirement eligibility, not only toward the benefit amount.**
§ 51.1-124.3 defines creditable service to include service purchased under § 51.1-142.2, and VRS
has confirmed this in print. A Plan 1 member at 27 years of service who buys three years reaches
the 30-year unreduced threshold immediately. That is why the benefit is valuable, and it is also
why it is expensive.

**The 24-month sentence is not military-specific.** It governs all eight non-military purchase
categories in (B)(1) as well. Any bill that edits that sentence in place reprices educational
leave, FMLA leave, out-of-state service and five other categories by accident. A military-only
price belongs in (B)(2), with a reciprocal exception in (B)(3). *(Note also that 2026 c. 563
relettered the whole section effective 1 July 2026: the target is subsection B, not the former
subsection A.)*

## 2. The gap this creates

![Virginia's price for identical service, by months since hire. The cliff's position is statutory and identical for every member; its height varies by profile from 2.0× to 3.2×.](assets/01_calendar_cliff.png)

For a Plan 1 member aged 55 with 27 years of service and a $91,700 salary, three years of military
service costs **$116,622** today and would have cost **$34,388** had the same member elected inside
the window.

**Who is on the wrong side of it.** The 24-month clock runs from first date of hire in a covered
position. Veterans enter state service later than the average hire — the Commonwealth's average
new-hire age is 39.1 years — and frequently after a private-sector interval. The window closes
before most mid-career hires have had a benefits conversation that reaches prior-service purchase.
This is a notice problem the Commonwealth created, and it is the part of the statute that is
genuinely hard to defend.

## 3. What other states do

Of the 20 states where we verified the price basis for prior (non-intervening) active-duty service
for general state employees:

| Price basis | States | Count |
|---|---|---:|
| Granted outright, at least to a defined cohort | W. Va. (pre-2015 hires), Md., Miss., Okla. (pre-2000 members), Ohio (narrow) | 5 |
| Below full actuarial cost, with **no cliff tied to hire date** | N.Y., Pa., Tex., Tenn., Ky., Utah, Fla. (closed cohort) | 7 |
| Full actuarial or actuarially neutral cost | N.C., S.C., Ohio (general), Ga., Colo., Wyo., W. Va. (post-2015 hires) | 7 |
| Below actuarial cost **only inside a short window measured from hire** | **Virginia** | **1** |

**The claim we make, and the one we do not.** Virginia is not unusual in charging for military
service — seven of the twenty charge full actuarial cost, and that is a respectable position.
Virginia is unusual in offering a cheap price and then withdrawing it on a calendar. Texas also
charges for delay, but through a published 10%-a-year escalator rather than a one-day cliff. We
make no claim about the 30 states we did not verify.

## 4. The proposed design

### 4.1 The price

For military service only:

> the member pays the **total normal cost** (member plus employer share) of the retirement plan under
> which the member is covered, applied to the member's **creditable compensation for the first 36
> months of VRS membership service**, plus **interest at six percent per annum, compounded
> annually**, from the end of that period to the date of payment — **and the 24-month election
> window does not apply**.

The structure is Pennsylvania's, at 71 Pa.C.S. § 5505(b). The interest rate is Virginia's own: §
6.2-301 sets the legal rate of interest at six percent, and § 6.2-302 sets the judgment rate at the
same figure.

**Why this is a better ask than a discount.** It is not a request to sell a benefit below its price
to a sympathetic class. It is the recovery, with interest, of the contributions the member never
had the chance to make — and at six percent the member pays **more than the cost VRS itself states
for a year of service credit**. It repairs the thing that is actually unfair, rather than carving
an exception around it.

::: warn
**One claim we do not make.** This is still a benefit enhancement and VRS may score it as one. The
difference between the bill's price and full actuarial-equivalent cost is a subsidy, and changing
the name of a price does not change its cost. The package asks the General Assembly to **fund**
that difference within a cap — not to accept that it is costless.
:::

### 4.2 The rate, and what it does and does not buy

| Rate written in the Code | Member pays, × approximate normal cost | Share of the current-law price | Verdict |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 4.00% (Pennsylvania's own rate) | 0.89× | 36% | **Rejected** — at 4.00% the member pays less than the original draft asked, which loses the sentence the design rests on |
| 5.00% | 1.15× | 47% | The floor, and only with the cap clause reopened in the same motion |
| **6.00%** | **1.50×** | **61%** | **Recommended.** These two figures are the **Pathways A+B** cohort; on the unreduced subdivision A 1 cohort, reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3 the member pays **1.42×** and **43%** |
| 6.75% (today's assumed rate of return) | 1.81× | 74% | Lower cost to the Commonwealth, but it takes the near-crossover member from a 23% discount to a 6% one. **The comparison is not stated in dollars here:** every absolute S8 figure is `[WITHHELD — S8 publication restriction]`, and its cost per participant may not be published before reconciliation |
| "the Board's assumed rate of return" | floats | floats | **Never draft it this way.** At 7.00% the price exceeds current law for the longest-serving members — 25 basis points above today's assumption |

**And the honest qualification.** The rate is **not** the Commonwealth's main cost control. The
cost of this design is dominated by members retiring earlier than VRS expects them to, and the
price the member pays does not reach that. The cost levers are the retirement deadline, the
completion requirement and the cohort — all three are drafting choices, and they are set out in
document 04 §6.

### 4.4 Scope

**State agency employees, Plan 1 and Plan 2.** § 51.1-142.2 is not limited to state employees, but
reaching teachers (160,949 actives) and political subdivisions raises the exposure by roughly an
order of magnitude and converts any local liability into an unfunded-mandate fight with VACo and
VML, independent of the policy's merits. Confining the pilot to state agency employees removes that
fight entirely: **no new duty falls on any locality, so there is nothing for a local government to
score.**

The exclusion of the Hybrid plan is **a scoring choice, not a principle**, and this package says
so. Plan 1 and Plan 2 are closed populations; that is what makes a four-year capped pilot
scoreable. And note the corollary we do **not** claim: Plan 2 members are about 7% of the eligible
cohort and reach eligibility by a different rule, so no tier in this bill is costless.

## 5. Who it reaches

The bill reaches **18.4 members over four years, range 9.2 to 30.7**, out of 81,274 covered members.
That count is measured on § 51.1-142.4 C as option (c), before the no-advance bar since added to the
text; the participant-count reconciliation prices the bar at about one election fewer. The two steps that do the narrowing — the share of members who are
veterans with purchasable service, and the share who would elect — are **analyst judgment**,
because 69% of the state workforce veteran-status field is recorded as unknown. That is why the
package shows a range and a cap rather than a point estimate, and why the Commonwealth's exposure
does not depend on getting the count right.

## 6. The workforce exchange, stated honestly

A member who retires earlier is replaced by a less senior hire at a lower loaded salary. That
differential is real. So is the cost of recruiting and ramping the replacement.

| Per participant | Conservative | **Central** | Optimistic |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Replacement salary differential | 8% | **18%** | 30% |
| Gross saving | $39,838 | **$72,437** | $111,555 |
| Recruitment and productivity ramp | −$29,010 | **−$26,400** | −$23,268 |
| **Net workforce effect** | **+$10,829** | **+$46,037** | **+$88,287** |
| Net cost to the trust per participating member — **repaired S1, not the recommended design** | −$64,678 | −$64,678 | −$64,678 |
| **Net effect on the Commonwealth** | **−$53,849** | **−$18,641** | **+$23,609** |

**At central assumptions the workforce effect covers about 71% of that cost, and no case we
regard as central covers all of it.** Break-even would need about 45 months of genuine
acceleration at an 18% differential.

**Two corrections travel with the $64,678, and the second makes this table less favourable, not
more.** It is **not** the pension cost of the purchased service: it is the net cost to the trust of
the whole election, of which only **28%** ($21,860) is service-credit content and **72%** ($54,891)
is the member commencing earlier. And it is **repaired S1's** anchor, not the recommended design's —
the recommended design costs **$71,813** per participant on the authorised basis, $7,135 more — so
**the exchange on the design this package actually recommends is worse than the rows above show.**
The recomputation on that anchor has not been produced by any source and is not attempted here.
Document 04 §9 carries the decomposition.

**So the package makes no savings claim, and the workforce figure is never netted against the cost
in any headline.** The independent-auditor record on state early-retirement incentives is
consistent and unfavourable: Pennsylvania −$12.4M, −$198M and −$68M across three programmes; New
York −$50M; West Virginia +$10M in annual retirement costs.

**What the design does deliver** is a *dated and reported* transition instead of an unmanaged one and
the first systematic data the Commonwealth would have on veteran service purchase and retirement
behaviour. **It does not deliver a backfill control** — subsection J was dropped, the bill has no
workforce-cost control, and the turnover argument rests on behaviour rather than on a provision. The pilot's evaluation is built to test the exchange; if it is
not there, the expiry handles it.
