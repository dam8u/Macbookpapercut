## 1. The question this model answers

> What is the present-value cost to the VRS trust, over a four-year pilot, of allowing a state
> agency employee in Plan 1 or Plan 2 — whom the purchase makes eligible to retire — to buy prior
> active-duty military service at the total normal cost of their plan applied to their first 36
> months of creditable compensation, plus six percent statutory interest, and requiring them to
> retire on completing the purchase?

- **Unit of analysis:** one electing member, aggregated to the trust, present-valued to the
  30 June 2025 valuation date.
- **Population:** State Employee plan only. Teachers and political subdivisions are out of scope.
- **Cohort:** elections are projected **monthly** across the 48 pilot months from membership
  history, with VRS's own published retirement and termination rates applied, and one election per
  member.
- **What is valued:** the basic single-life pension benefit only.

## 3. The formulas, written out

**3.1 The member's price under the bill.**

    price = total normal cost rate
          × (average annual compensation over the first 36 months of VRS membership service)
          × (years purchased)
          × (1 + 0.06)^n

where `n` is the number of years from the end of that 36-month period to the date of payment. The
first-36-month compensation is reconstructed from the member's current salary by walking VRS's own
service-based salary-increase scale backwards.

**3.2 Why the price collapses to two terms.** VRS defines its approximate-normal-cost rate as "the
average cost of one year of VRS service credit" — the total member-plus-employer normal cost, which
is exactly what the Pennsylvania formula charges. The two rates are the same number and cancel:

    price ÷ approximate-normal-cost price = (first-36-month comp ÷ current comp) × (1 + i)^n
                                                  salary term (<1)      interest term (>1)

**3.3 The liability.** One formula, applied twice and differenced:

    PV(member | retirement age R, purchased years y)
        = multiplier × service_at_R × AFC(R) × annuity(R) × v^(R − age) × survival(age → R)

    liability to the trust = PV(retirement under the design) − PV(retirement without it)
                             − member payments

The counterfactual retirement age is **VRS's own expected retirement date**, derived from its
published retirement and termination rates — not an assumption of our own. That single change is
the repair described in §13, and it is what makes acceleration, rather than the discount, the
dominant cost.

*(This table is the full A + B cohort on a six-month deadline, which is the comparison basis used
across the scenario set. **The member-price columns, 1.50× and 61%, are that A + B cohort and must
always be labelled as such;** the unreduced subdivision A 1 cohort, reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3 pays 1.42× and 43% and is priced in §2.

**member's price**, which is the column that is published.)*

**Why 6.00% and not another number.**

1. It is **Virginia's own legal rate of interest** — Va. Code § 6.2-301, with § 6.2-302 setting the
   judgment rate at the same six percent. The bill borrows a formula from Pennsylvania and a rate
   from the Code of Virginia, and has to defend neither as novel.
2. At six percent the member pays **more than VRS's own stated cost of a year of service credit**.
   That is the single sentence the design rests on, and no lower rate keeps it: at 4.00% the member
   pays **0.89×** — less than the original draft asked for.
3. It is clear of the crossover. The statutory rate at which the bill's price would equal the
   current-law price runs from **7.00%** for the tightest of 96 profiles to 10.39% for the loosest.
   **Six percent leaves 100 basis points of headroom on the tightest profile**, and not one of the
   15 profiles nearest the crossover is worse off. They pay **68%–77% of the current-law price,
   median 72%**.
4. **Never draft it as "the Board's assumed rate of return."** That floats, it is 6.75% today, and
   at 7.00% the bill becomes worse than doing nothing for its longest-serving beneficiaries.

::: warn
**The rate is not the cost control, and the package must not present it as one.** On the repaired
basis the statutory rate is only the **sixth-order driver** of the Commonwealth's cost. Moving from
6.00% to 6.75% buys about twelve percentage points of ratio and costs the near-crossover member
most of their discount. The cost levers are the retirement deadline, the completion requirement and
the cohort — see §6.
:::

## 6. Where the cost actually comes from, and the levers that reach it

The cost is **retirement-date acceleration**. Under the bill the electing member retires about
**57 months** earlier than VRS's own rates expect them to. The member's payment does not touch
that, which is why the price is the wrong dial.

**The retirement-deadline ladder**, the unreduced subdivision A 1 cohort, reached through § 51.1-153 A, B 2 or B 3, 6.00% statutory interest:

| Retirement deadline after completing the purchase | Acceleration | Cost to the trust, central | Range |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Same month the credit posts (rung 0) | 54.9 months | $1,567,024 | $0.78M – $2.61M |
| **As drafted — the first of the month after the credit posts (rung 1)** | **53.9 months** | **$1,547,336** | $0.77M – $2.58M |
| 6 months | 48.9 months | $1,446,675 | $0.72M – $2.41M |
| **12 months — the authorised rung** | **42.9 months** | **$1,321,112** | **$0.66M – $2.20M** |
| 18 months | 36.9 months | $1,190,510 | $0.60M – $1.98M |
| 24 months | 30.9 months | $1,055,048 | $0.53M – $1.76M |

**The first two rows are the labelling correction.** Earlier versions printed a single
"immediately (as drafted)" row carrying the **rung-0** figure. The drafted text requires retirement
on the **first of the month after** the credit posts, which is **rung 1**. Both are printed here and
the drafted setting is the rung-1 row; every percentage below is measured from it.

**The deadline is the largest cost control in the drafter's hands, and it is still open.** Moving
from the drafted setting to 24 months is **32% below** it **on this ladder** — $1,055,048
against $1,547,336 — without changing the price the veteran pays by a dollar, and without weakening
the retirement requirement in substance: the member still must retire.

**What the 32% is, stated exactly, because it is the most-repeated cost claim in this package.** The
ladder prices **one design at six settings of one parameter** — the same eligibility rule, the same
price, the same subsidy limits, the same cohort — and it **holds the participant count constant**.
The count is **rung-invariant by measurement, not by assumption**: the largest difference in any
participant count between rungs, across seven designs and three take-up cases, is **0.0000000000**.
The retirement deadline moves what the trust pays, not who elects. But no source produces a
participant count for a *behavioural* response to the deadline either. So the 32% is a **modelled
conditional result: a 32% reduction in modelled present-value cost to the trust at constant
participation.** It is **not** a demonstrated 32% reduction in total programme expenditure, and it is
not a 32% reduction in the appropriation, which is a fixed $3,000,000 either way. A longer deadline
plausibly changes who elects, and that effect is unmeasured. The **15%** between the drafted rung and
the authorised 12-month rung carries the same reading. The two arguments against a longer deadline are both weak: the windfall case is the
*cheap* case, not the expensive one; and enforcement is already solved in the draft, which
re-values the credit at the current-law price and refunds the difference if the member does not
retire.

Two further levers, measured on the full cohort: a **longer completion requirement** cuts cost
(paying over 36 months rather than in a lump sum is worth an 83% swing — faster completion is
dearer), and the **cohort definition** is already set to its narrowest form. **The 83% swing reads
the same way as the 32%:** one design at two settings of one parameter, participation held constant,
and no participant count per setting exists.

## 7. The cap, and what it actually does

A cap is only meaningful as an **admission rule**: VRS certifies that enough of the appropriation
remains to cover a member's estimated cost *before* accepting the election, and wait-lists beyond
that. As a reporting limit it does nothing.

**This section is rebuilt in this version, not amended, and here is why.** The previous version
priced the cap against **cost to the trust**. Subsection D 2 caps **certified subsidy** plus a
contingency, and the cap limit is cumulative with the contingency inside it (`bill-text-s8`, D 2 and
D 3). Those are different quantities, so the previous conclusion — *"$3.0M is the smallest round
number that makes the cap not bind"* — was reached on the wrong measure and does not survive. It is
withdrawn in §13 and is not replaced by a restatement on the same measure.

**What the appropriation actually does, on the quantity the bill caps.**

| Take-up | Share of the $3,000,000 committed, `[20]`% contingency inside | Does the cap bind? |
|---|---:|---|
| low | **39%** | no |
| **central** | **78%** | **no — the appropriation funds every election the bill admits** |
| high | **130%** | **yes — the appropriation binds and rations** |

**At the central estimate the $3,000,000 funds every election the bill admits, and at the top of the
range the appropriation binds and rations.** Those three percentages are **modelled demand measured
against the cap, not observed utilisation** — nothing has been administered, so there is no
utilisation to observe. The dollar aggregate of certified subsidy behind them is **$1,954,680**
reserved at the central estimate, which is **$2,345,616** charged against the cap once the `[20]`%
contingency is counted inside it — 78% of $3,000,000.

**And one thing the 12-month window does not do.** The Commonwealth **reserves** $1,954,680 nominal
while the trust **pays** $1,668,678 nominal; the $286,001 difference is the window's entire effect.
D 2 meters the **reservation**, certified on the anticipated earliest qualifying retirement date,
which the window does not move. **Any statement that the 12-month rung eases the $3,000,000 is
wrong**, and none is made in this package.

**The statute turns members away in two places before the money does, and they belong first.**

| Refusal channel | Members refused, central take-up, of a 20.65 population |
|---|---:|
| Maximum purchasable amount (subsection C clause (i) / B 2 entitlement) | **0.00** |
| The zero-month bar in subsection C and the no-advance bar in clause (vii) of "eligible member", **together** | about **1.0** |
| The **$170,000** per-member subsidy ceiling | about **1.2** |
| The $3,000,000 appropriation, at the central estimate | **0.0** |

*(The split is settled on the authorised basis and is stated here: the zero-barred set is a strict
**subset** of the no-advance band, so the **zero-month bar refuses 0.0000** and the **no-advance bar
1.0148**. The bar MOO-87 added subsumes the screen the bill already had. The earlier figure of 0.46
on the zero-month bar was an artefact of the superseded cohort and is withdrawn.)*

**The maximum purchasable amount refuses nobody, and that is a structural finding rather than a
zero.** Minimum state service anywhere in the cohort is 13.5 years against a maximum purchasable
amount of 3.0 years, so the subdivision B 2 entitlement is the binding limb for 100% of the cohort,
and a member with too little state service is assigned to the Hybrid tier and excluded by clause
(iii) of "eligible member" before this limb is reached.

**One thing this package has to say that it did not plan to.** At the central estimate the bill's own
limits refuse about 2.2 members between them and the appropriation refuses none. A sentence about
what "the appropriation funds" is therefore not the main story about who gets nothing, which is why
the zero bar and the ceiling are set out ahead of the cap here and in documents 00, 01, 02 and 06.
*(One figure-reading note for the record, flagged rather than resolved: the authorising document
states as a general proposition that "the statute itself turns away more members than money does",
and its own table supports that on option (a) — 7.9 against 0.4 — but not on the authorised option
(c), where the eligibility bars refuse 1.0 against the ceiling's 1.2. The ordering instruction stands
on its own figures, because both bars refuse members at the central estimate and the appropriation
does not. The general proposition is not asserted in this package.)*

**The per-member subsidy ceiling is $170,000**, board-confirmed, on the reservation basis subsection
D 2 certifies. **The distribution the ceiling screens is the live set as reserved** — mean
$110,861, median $109,428, 90th percentile **$162,314**, maximum $209,820 — and the ceiling refuses
the most expensive **4.74%** of it. Two caveats travel with that figure and both are required. First,
**the bill still brackets `[$100,000]`**; the replacement is directed and is not yet in the text.
Second, **a refusal is not a trim.** The whole certified subsidy of a refused member is **$183,577**
central against **$9,147** of excess over the ceiling — a factor of twenty. Anyone who assumes the
ceiling shaves the overage off an otherwise-funded election is wrong by twenty times; it removes the
election. **$80,000 is withdrawn:** it excludes 100% of the cohort the bill admits, whose cheapest
election is $81,278.

**For its first two fiscal years the bill costs $375,000 of non-general fund money and nothing
else.** The systems charge lands in a year in which no election can be made, because the effective
date coincides with a fiscal-year boundary and no programme year is split. That is a useful fact in
a money committee: the largest single number in the first biennium is administration, not benefit.

The 47% general fund share is applied to the amortisation of trust cost only. **The $375,000
implementation allowance is non-general fund and is never multiplied by the general fund share.**

**No local cost and no unfunded mandate.** The pilot is confined to state agency employees, so no
new duty falls on a locality and there is nothing for a local government to score.

## 10. The workforce exchange, measured and not claimed

A participant who retires earlier is replaced by a less senior hire. The loaded salary differential
is real money; so is the cost of recruiting and ramping the replacement, and that cost is paid
first. Per participant, on the full-cohort acceleration of 34 months:

| Case | Replacement differential | Gross saving | Recruitment + ramp | **Net workforce effect** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Conservative *(leads)* | 8% | $39,838 | −$29,010 | **+$10,829** |
| **Central** | **18%** | **$72,437** | **−$26,400** | **+$46,037** |
| Optimistic | 30% | $111,555 | −$23,268 | **+$88,287** |

**And the honest conclusion, which is why no savings claim appears anywhere in this package:** the
cost set against the workforce effect on the same basis is **$64,678 per participant**, and its
identity is now **confirmed** — it is the same quantity as the scoreboard's cost per participant for
repaired S1, from the same run and the same field, reproducing to a tenth of a cent.

**Two corrections travel with it and both are required.** First, **it is not the pension cost of the
purchased service**, which is how earlier versions labelled it. It is the **net cost to the trust per
participating member of the whole election**, and only **28%** of it is purchased service:

| per participating member, repaired S1 | |
|---|---:|
| gross liability increase | $76,751 |
| — of which service-credit content | **$21,860 (28%)** |
| — of which earlier-commencement content | **$54,891 (72%)** |
| less member payment | −$12,073 |
| **= net cost to the trust** | **$64,678** |

A reader who takes the old label at face value concludes the Commonwealth is paying $64,678 for four
years of service credit. It is paying $21,860 for the credit and $54,891 for the member retiring
earlier, less what the member pays.

Second, **it is S1's anchor, not the bill's.** S1 is the drafted-at-approximate-normal-cost design
this package does **not** recommend. The recommended design's figure is **$71,813** per participant
on the authorised basis, and $64,678 is also a **6-month-deadline** figure where the authorised rung
is 12 months. **So the 71% below is measured against S1's cost, not against the recommended design's,
and the exchange on the recommended design is worse than this table shows** — the per-participant
cost is $7,135 higher. The recomputation on the recommended design's own anchor has not been
produced by any source and is not attempted here. At the central differential the workforce effect
covers about **71%** of S1's cost and no case covers all of it. Break-even would need about
**45 months** of genuine acceleration at an 18% differential.

**The workforce figure is never netted against the pension cost in any headline in this package**,
and no case is added to any cost figure. Two further reasons for that discipline:

- The independent-audit record on state early-retirement incentives is unfavourable: Pennsylvania
  −$12.4M, −$198M and −$68M across three programmes; New York −$50M; West Virginia +$10M in annual
  retirement costs. A 1988 multi-state survey found the states that did save were those that
  **restricted rehiring** or **required certified savings before implementation**. Both devices are
  in this design and neither is scored.
- The Commonwealth's own workforce data cuts against a retention framing: service retirements are
  already running above average, and 5.1% of the classified workforce can retire today.

What the Commonwealth gets that is not a dollar: a dated, reported and controlled transition rather
than an unmanaged one, and the first systematic data it would have on veteran service purchase and
retirement behaviour — 69% of the relevant workforce field is currently recorded as unknown.

## 12. External check

VRS's fiscal impact statement on SB 419 (2026) implies a cost of 20.4% of salary per year of
granted service; adjusted for plan richness to a State-plan equivalent, 11.9%. This model runs
**above** that benchmark on a like-for-like population, not below — so it is not flattering the
proposal. The gap is consistent with this model excluding the Health Insurance Credit and Group
Life, which VRS would include and which push in the same direction.

**Reported as a disagreement, not smoothed.**

## 14. Reproducing these numbers

The model is Python and emits every table in this document as a CSV.

```
python3 v2_main.py                   # the repaired model — every figure in this document
python3 export_browser_model_v2.py   # the data behind the interactive model
python3 make_charts.py               # the figures
```

The interactive version of this analysis — the member price calculator, the rate dial, the deadline
ladder, the cap explorer, the sensitivity chart and the workforce break-even — is the single file
`vrs-financial-model.html`, which opens in any browser with no network connection and no install.

**It is withheld from this version, and the reason is the same rule that governs everything else
here.** Its data arrays are embedded at build time and they are the **superseded** family: the model
returns 18.9 participants and a $1,411,988 aggregate on every tab, against the 18.4 and $1,321,112
this document now prints. A reader moving its dials would get a second answer to every question the
documents answer. Re-emitting those arrays on the authorised basis is a model run, not an editorial
change, so it is commissioned rather than improvised. The file is kept, unaltered, at
`03-financial-model-interactive/superseded/`.
